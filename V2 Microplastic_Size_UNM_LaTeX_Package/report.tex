\begin{abstract}\noindent
This report answers two questions from the uploaded Bibliometrix dataset (\texttt{Bibliometrix\_\allowbreak Filtered.RData}): (1) what microplastic size is most commonly reported in rivers and ponds, with a focus on the Semenyih River; and (2) which papers are affiliated with the University of Nottingham Malaysia (UNM), what methods they used, and what they found. Every count below comes from a documented filter applied to the records in the file, and the filter code and output tables are included in the package. In the screened river/lake/pond abstracts that state a dominant size class (n\,=\,616), about 87.3\% report a class below 1\,mm. UNM contributes nine records, of which five are primary microplastic studies in the Langat--Semenyih catchment. \flag{Update:} the full texts of two papers (Chen et al.\ 2021 on the Langat River and the 2026 review of Malaysian rivers) are now used, which supplies UNM instruments (Leica EZ4 stereomicroscope, PerkinElmer Frontier FTIR), a 53\um{} sieve cut-off and a reported visual-identification accuracy of 96\%. The other UNM papers remain abstract-only, and no source gives a size histogram for the Semenyih River itself.
\end{abstract}

How the last step works: for each abstract that passed the numeric-size step, sentences were kept only if they contained a dominance word (dominant, majority, most, predominant, mainly, prevalent, etc.) and a size expression. Sentences that merely define microplastics ($<$5\,mm, 1\um--5\,mm) or give a study's search range were removed. The first remaining expression was converted to millimetres and assigned a class by its \emph{upper bound}: $\le$0.1\,mm, 0.1--0.5\,mm, 0.5--1\,mm (this class also holds generic ``$<$1\,mm'' statements) and $>$1\,mm up to 5\,mm.

\subsection{Filter for Question 2 (University of Nottingham Malaysia)}
Six affiliation fields (\texttt{C1}, \texttt{affiliations}, \texttt{AU\_UN}, \texttt{RP}, \texttt{AU1\_UN}, \texttt{organization}) were searched for ``Nottingham'' within 80--200 characters of ``Malaysia'', ``Semenyih'', ``Broga'' or ``Selangor'', plus the abbreviation UNMC. This returned \textbf{9 records}. For comparison, 33 records mention Nottingham in \texttt{C1}, but most belong to the UK campus, Nottingham Ningbo China, Nottingham Trent University or the British Geological Survey, and were excluded. A cross-check on author names (Chen HL, Selvam SB, Gibbins C, Ting KN, Ng YS, Show PL) found two further records not in the set: a Ningbo-campus review and an unrelated schlieren-imaging paper; neither lists a Malaysian Nottingham address.

\section{Question 1 -- Literature review: common microplastic size in rivers and ponds}
\subsection{What the screen shows}
Among the 616 river/lake/pond abstracts that state a dominant class, sub-millimetre particles dominate (Figure~\ref{fig:bars}). Classes by share: $<$100\um{} 16.2\%, 100--500\um{} 37.5\%, 0.5--1\,mm 33.6\%, and 1--5\,mm 12.7\% (counts 100, 231, 207, 78). \subsection{Why the answer depends on the method}
Reported size is partly a function of what the method can see. Within the UNM group's own fish/invertebrate study, visual inspection covered 0.1--5\,mm while a new automated method covered 0.004--0.1\,mm and returned substantially higher counts, so visual work under-estimates small particles \cite{ng24}. A Perak River study using fluorescence and FTIR microscopy found most particles at 1--10\um{} \cite{hassan25}, and a review of Malaysian rivers notes that detection limits fell to 1--10\um{} as the field moved from visual microscopy to $\mu$FTIR and Py-GC/MS \cite{bouida26}. The headline ``most are below 1\,mm'' is therefore robust, but the split between 100--500\um{} and 0.5--1\,mm depends on mesh and instrument.

\section{Question 1.1 -- Focus diagram: microplastic size in the Semenyih River}
\flag{What the sources can and cannot say.} The Semenyih River is one of the two similar-sized upper sub-catchments that merge to form the Langat; Chen et al.\ 2021 placed sites 1--3 on the Semenyih River (site 1 about 1\,km below the Semenyih dam, site 2 at the upstream edge of Semenyih town, site 3 just downstream) and sites 4--8 along the Langat mainstem \cite{chen21a} (full text). Sites 1--3 had significantly lower concentrations than the downstream sites, and microplastics were still found at site 1 near the dam. The Semenyih-specific study of Chen et al.\ 2023 sampled eight sites on the upper Semenyih River, five replicates of water and bed on three dates (160 samples), with $3.12\pm2.49$ particles/L in water, $6027\pm16{,}586$ particles/m$^{2}$ on the bed and fibres dominant \cite{chen23}. Neither source reports a size histogram. What they do fix is the \emph{counted range}: both pass samples through a 53\um{} sieve and count by stereomicroscope (8--35$\times$), so particles smaller than about 53\um{} are outside the data, and the review lists stereomicroscopy as typically reliable above 300\um{} \cite{bouida26}. The diagram therefore shows this counted range for the Semenyih work next to the Langat and Selangor size results and the UNM method windows.

\noindent\textbf{Accuracy.} The only detection-accuracy figure found is the Langat study's FTIR check: 85 randomly picked particles were scanned and the visual identification was reported as 96\% accurate; the polymers were PET 43\%, HDPE 21\%, LDPE 13\%, PP 8\%, EVA 8\%, PVDC 5\% and PS 4\%, and five particles were non-synthetic (cotton, viscose, silica) \cite{chen21a}. This test validates identification, not size or recovery: no spiked-particle recovery rate or detection limit is given. The review adds that stereomicroscopy has low polymer-identification reliability unless confirmed by a hot-needle test, and that its usual size limit is about 300\um{} \cite{bouida26}; the group's later automated method reaches down to 4\um{} \cite{ng24}, and a deep-learning counter reaches human-level counts \cite{lee22}. Instrument details for the Ng, Lee and Chen 2022/2023 papers still need their own full texts.

\subsection{Summary of UNM findings}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,labelindent=0pt,style=unboxed]
\item[Abundance.] Langat mainstem mean $4.39\pm5.11$ particles/L over 508 samples, rising more than threefold from the rural upper reach to the urban lower reach (sites 7 and 8 highest); the two industrial tributaries averaged $45.86\pm24.76$ and reached 90 particles/L \cite{chen21a} (full text). The river was estimated to convey about 5 billion particles/day half of the time and about 30 billion/day for 10\% of the time. The Semenyih River carried $3.12\pm2.49$ particles/L and $6027\pm16{,}586$ particles/m$^2$ on the bed \cite{chen23}. The review cautions that the very high Langat values of other groups (up to 2012 items/L) reflect finer detection (0.2\um{} filter, \(\mu\)FTIR) rather than a pure environmental difference \cite{bouida26}.
\item[Type and origin.] In the Langat, fibres were 96\%, fragments 3\%, films 1\% and beads under 1\%, mostly secondary; domestic textile washing and aerial fallout are the likely sources in the near-forest upper reach, and clothing-factory drains in the tributaries \cite{chen21a}. In the Semenyih, fibres dominated water and bed with more fragments on the bed \cite{chen23}, and the review reports PE and polyester there and a ``sink danger'' risk category \cite{bouida26}. Animals held a more even mix (about 35\% fibres, 43\% fragments, 22\% film) \cite{ng24}.
\item[Drivers.] Concentrations averaged $1.53\pm0.87$ particles/L at low flow and $17.83\pm6.73$ particles/L at elevated discharge, tripled during a June 2020 flood, and tracked suspended sediment (quantile regression pseudo-$R^2$ 0.1 for the median and 0.65 for the 90th quantile), so turbidity sensors could estimate loads \cite{chen21a}. Road culverts delivered $42.20\pm35.29$ particles/L, up to seven times residential ($8.53\pm9.91$) and industrial ($5.67\pm4.88$) drains; atmospheric deposition was $76.07\pm32.85$ particles/m$^2$/day; WWTP outflows were similar in concentration to uncontrolled drains \cite{chen22}.
\item[Biota.] 95\% of fish and 44\% of macroinvertebrates held larger microplastics, with loads linked to feeding group and body size \cite{ng24}.
\item[Monitoring advice.] Water-only monitoring may misrank bed contamination, and single bed samples are inadequate \cite{chen23}; high-resolution processing is needed for small organisms \cite{ng24}.
\item[Regional context.] The group's review concludes that data in Southeast Asian fresh waters are limited, with Malaysia a focus, and lists transport, fate and effects as gaps \cite{chen21b}. Show PL's reviews cover health, socio-economic effects and phytoplankton interactions \cite{mofijur21,alowaisy26}.
\end{description}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Text-mining of abstracts (Question 1): papers that report size only in the full text are missed, and a few off-target hits (for example soil or snow studies that mention a river) remain in the 616.
\item Classes are assigned by upper bound, so a 300--1000\um{} statement falls in the 0.5--1\,mm class.
\item The Semenyih size histogram is still unknown: the full text of Chen et al.\ 2023 itself is needed. Methods for that paper and for Chen et al.\ 2022 are second-hand, from the review's summary table \cite{bouida26}.\item The Chen et al.\ 2021 accuracy figure is quoted as stated (96\%); 5 of the 85 tested particles were non-synthetic, which is arithmetically 94.1\% correct. The review's Table~3 lists granules as the dominant Semenyih shape while its text and the Semenyih paper say fibres; the review's text also gives the Langat range as 1811--4157 items/L where the source abstract says 181--4157.
\item Starting count is 10,897, not 10,833 (Section~1).
\end{itemize}
